Text 1

In a far-off forest a sweetgum was born one hundred and forty-six years ago. It has five million three hundred and thirty-three thousand five hundred and forty-three leaves, spread over two thousand three hundred and forty-two branches, which meet in a trunk thirty-four metres tall and reach, with the crown, forty.

It is a deciduous tree. Its leaves are shaped like stars, with three to five points, and in autumn they turn orange, red, almost violet.

From its grey, cracked bark comes a resin that smells of flowers. The Maya and the Aztecs used it as incense in their temples, as medicine, or as currency.

A sweetgum can live up to four hundred years. This one has lived a hundred and forty-six: barely a third of what it could live on this earth.

That, too, must be borne in mind, further on in this story.

Text 2

The bird is a swallow. She was born in a nest of mud and straw that her parents built in barely ten days.

She was the last of five eggs to break the shell: blind, almost featherless and tiny as a walnut. A breath of existence.

Nineteen days after she was born she made her first flight. She has not stopped since.

A swallow can fly at more than a hundred kilometres an hour when she has to, and cross twelve thousand kilometres twice a year: once south, fleeing the cold; once north, back to where she was born.

She eats only insects caught in mid-flight, like bats do. She never stops, not even to eat. When she met the tree the swallow had already seen five springs and flown, there and back, more than a hundred and twenty thousand kilometres.

They say you can reckon these birds' lives by the length and the symmetry of their tails. Most do not reach four years. This one had already won a few off that account.

Text 3

That day the bird came to the tree to begin her nest.
The tree accepted.
But on one condition.

(tree) —Stay. I have room among my branches for you and for any nest you care to make.

I will give you my stillness.

(bird) —And I will give you the world.
When I come back at the end of each season I will tell you everything I saw.

So began this relationship between what has roots and what has wings.
